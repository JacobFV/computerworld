Placeholder text (also called filler text or dummy text) is text that occupies the space where real copy will later appear, so that a page's layout and typography can be judged before the words exist.[1] The best-known specimen is lorem ipsum, a scrambled passage of Latin that has been in use since the sixteenth century.[2]

The purpose of placeholder text is to look like language without reading like it. A reviewer looking at a draft should notice the columns, the margins and the rhythm of the type rather than the meaning of the words. Text that is too regular, such as a single repeated word, gives a false impression of colour on the page; text that is too meaningful draws the eye away from the design.[3]

History[edit]

A type specimen sheet showing several sizes of the same face, set in filler text so the reader looks at the letterforms.

Printers in the sixteenth century needed pages of type to show prospective customers, and they needed them to be about nothing in particular. A scrambled passage taken from a classical text served the purpose: it had the letter frequencies and word lengths of ordinary Latin, but it no longer said anything a customer could argue with.[4]

The practice survived the move to phototypesetting and then to desktop publishing. A widely used page-layout program shipped with the passage built in, which is why the same fragment appears in so many mock-ups from the 1980s onward.[5] Later tools offered generators that produce any quantity of similar text on demand.

Forms[edit]

Lorem ipsum

The canonical passage begins lorem ipsum dolor sit amet and runs for several sentences. It is not grammatical Latin: words have been cut, joined and shuffled so that no sentence can be translated.[2]

Pangrams

A pangram is a sentence that uses every letter of the alphabet at least once. The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog is the usual English example; it is short enough to show every glyph of a face on one line, which makes it a favourite of font menus and type specimens.[6] Other languages have their own: a German one about twelve boxers, a French one about a whisky-drinking judge.
